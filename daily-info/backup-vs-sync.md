"Is it backed up?" is an easy question to get wrong. Many people believe their photos and files are backed up because they're "in the cloud", only to find out after an accident that what they had was sync, not a backup. The two sound similar, often live in the same apps, and even use the same cloud storage. But they're built for different jobs, and the difference matters most at exactly the moment something goes wrong.

This guide explains what sync does, what a backup does, where each one protects you and where it doesn't, and how to set things up so your photos, contacts and files are safe.

## The short answer

Sync keeps things the same everywhere. When you add, change or delete something on one device, sync copies that change to your other devices and the cloud, so everything matches.

A backup keeps a separate copy for recovery. It saves a copy of your data so that if something is lost, deleted or damaged, you can bring the earlier version back.

Sync is for convenience and a backup is for recovery. You usually need both, and problems start when one is mistaken for the other.

| | Sync | Backup |
|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | Keep data identical across devices | Keep a copy you can restore from |
| If you add something | It appears everywhere | It's added to the backup next time it runs |
| If you delete something | It's deleted everywhere, usually after a spell in the trash | The backup copy stays, so you can restore it |
| If your phone is lost | Your synced data is still on your other devices and online | You can restore your data onto a new phone |
| If a file gets damaged | The damaged version can sync everywhere | An earlier, undamaged copy can be restored |
{: .guide-table}

## What sync does

Sync, short for synchronisation, is designed to make your data follow you. Add a contact on your phone, and a moment later it's on your tablet and your computer. Edit a document on your laptop, and the updated version is waiting on your phone. Take a photo, and it appears in your photo library everywhere you're signed in.

This is useful. It means you're never working from an out-of-date copy, and it means losing a single device doesn't mean losing the data on it, because the same data lives on your other devices and in the cloud.

On most phones, several things sync by default when you're signed in to your main account:

- Contacts usually sync to your Google account on Android or iCloud on an iPhone.
- Calendars sync the same way, so events appear on every device.
- Photos can sync through a photo service such as Google Photos or iCloud Photos, if you've turned it on.
- Notes, reminders and browser bookmarks often sync through your account.
- Files in cloud drives such as Google Drive, iCloud Drive, OneDrive or Dropbox sync between your devices.

Sync's job is to make every copy identical. That is what makes it useful, and, as the next section shows, it is also the risk.

## What a backup does

A backup is designed to let you go back. It takes a copy of your data at a point in time and keeps it separately, so that later, if something happens to the original, you can restore that copy.

The important difference is that a backup doesn't mirror what's on your phone right now. If you delete a photo from your phone, a backup keeps its copy. If a file becomes damaged, the backup still has the version from before it was damaged. If you lose your phone, the backup has everything it held at the last backup, ready to restore onto a new one.

A good backup has three qualities:

1. It's separate. It lives somewhere other than the device it protects, so whatever happens to the phone doesn't happen to the backup.
2. It's current. It runs regularly, so it includes recent photos and changes as well as older ones.
3. It's restorable. You can get your data back out of it, onto the same phone or a new one, when you need it.

A backup that's out of date, or that you can't restore from, isn't much use. Check yours now and then, as well as setting it up.

## When sync can work against you

Because sync's job is to keep everything identical, it copies your mistakes just as faithfully as your good changes. There are a few situations where that turns a small problem into a big one.

### Deleting something by mistake

This is the most common. You clear out photos to free up space, or tidy your contacts, and accidentally remove something you wanted. With sync, that deletion is copied to the cloud and to your other devices. The photo or contact disappears from every device.

Most services soften this with a trash folder, which gives you a window to change your mind. But once that window closes, the item is gone from every synced copy.

### A problem with a file

If a document or photo becomes damaged, or is overwritten with the wrong version, sync will happily copy the damaged version to every device. Some cloud drives keep older versions of files, which can help, but that history is usually limited, and it's rarely available for photos and contacts.

### A problem with an app or account

If an app misbehaves, or someone gains access to your account and deletes things, sync spreads the change. A backup that's stored separately, and that can't be overwritten by the same change, is what protects you here.

### Storage running out

When a synced cloud account runs out of space, new photos and files may stop syncing, sometimes without much warning. You carry on taking photos believing they're safe, when in fact they're only on your phone.

## Trash folders and version history

Most sync services include some safety nets. Here is what they are and how long they last.

Trash and recently deleted folders. When you delete a photo or file, many services move it to a trash folder for a set period before removing it permanently. iCloud Photos keeps deleted photos in Recently Deleted for 30 days. Google Photos keeps items in its trash for 60 days if they were backed up, and 30 days if they weren't. Cloud drives such as Google Drive, iCloud Drive, OneDrive and Dropbox also keep deleted files for a limited time, often around 30 days on personal accounts.

Version history. Some cloud drives keep earlier versions of files, so you can roll back a document to how it was before a change. This is more common for documents than for photos, and the number of versions or length of history is usually limited.

These features are helpful, and they rescue many accidentally deleted photos. They are still not a backup. They only last a limited time, they usually empty themselves automatically, and they can be emptied by hand, deliberately or by accident. If you only discover a missing photo two months later, the safety net may already be gone.

## Which of your phone's data is synced, and which is backed up

For each kind of data on your phone, find out whether it's synced, backed up, both or neither. The answer varies with your settings, but the general picture looks like this:

- Contacts and calendars are usually synced to your account. That protects you from losing your phone, but a contact deleted by mistake is deleted everywhere.
- Photos and videos are synced if you use a photo service like Google Photos or iCloud Photos. They may also be included in a separate backup, depending on the service and your settings.
- Messages are usually covered by your phone's own device backup (Google's backup on Android, or iCloud Backup on an iPhone), and chat apps like WhatsApp have their own separate chat backups.
- App data may be synced to the app's own account, included in your phone's device backup, or not saved anywhere at all. It varies app by app.
- Files you've downloaded often aren't synced or backed up unless you've saved them to a cloud drive.

A useful exercise is to go through that list for your own phone and fill in the answer for each one. The gaps you find are the things you'd lose if your phone disappeared tomorrow.

## Is cloud storage a backup?

Whether cloud storage counts as a backup depends on how you use it.

If you use a cloud drive as a synced folder, where files on your phone and in the cloud are kept identical, it behaves like sync. Delete a file in one place, and it's deleted in the other.

If you use cloud storage to keep a separate copy of your data, one that isn't automatically changed when you delete or edit things on your phone, it behaves like a backup.

The same service can often do both, depending on your settings. That's why it's worth reading the options in your photo and cloud apps, and why the useful question is "if I delete it from my phone, what happens to the copy in the cloud?"

## The 3-2-1 rule, made simple

A common rule of thumb for backups is 3-2-1: keep three copies of important data, on two different types of storage, with one copy kept somewhere else.

For a phone, it works out like this:

1. Copy one: the data on your phone itself.
2. Copy two: a backup in the cloud, kept separately from your phone and off-site by nature.
3. Copy three: a copy on a computer or external drive at home.

You don't need to follow it to the letter for everything. For your most precious data, usually photos and videos of people and moments that can't be repeated, it's a sensible goal. For less critical things, a phone plus one good backup is often enough.

## How to set up both

You don't have to choose between sync and backup. The safest setup uses both.

Use sync for convenience. Keep your contacts, calendars and notes syncing to your main account, so they're always up to date on every device, and so losing a phone doesn't mean losing them.

Use a backup for recovery. Make sure your photos, videos, contacts and app data are also backed up somewhere that keeps its own copy, one that isn't wiped out when you delete something on your phone.

Keep your phone's own device backup turned on. On Android, this is usually found in Settings under Google or System, then Backup. On an iPhone, it's iCloud Backup, in Settings under your name, then iCloud. It covers settings and some app data that other backups may not.

Back up your chat apps separately. WhatsApp and similar apps have their own backup settings. Check they're turned on and that the last backup date is recent.

## Signs your backup has stopped

Backups rarely fail with an error. They stop, and nothing tells you until you look for something that isn't there. Watch for these signs:

- A "storage full" message from your cloud account. When an account runs out of space, new photos and files usually stop being backed up. It's easy to dismiss the notification without realising what it means.
- You changed your password recently. Some backup apps need you to sign in again after a password change, and pause until you do.
- You got a new phone. Backups set up on an old phone don't always carry over their settings. It's common to restore everything onto a new phone and forget to turn backup back on.
- Battery saver or data saver is always on. Power- and data-saving modes often pause background activity, including backups, until the phone is charging or on Wi-Fi.
- An app update changed the settings. Occasionally an update resets options, such as which folders are included in a backup.

If any of these apply to you, open your backup app and check the date of the last completed backup. It takes a moment, and finding a problem now is far better than finding it after something is lost.

## When you have more than one device

Sync becomes more powerful, and slightly more risky, the more devices you have. A family sharing an account across a phone, a tablet and a computer has three places where something can be deleted, and sync will copy that deletion to all of them.

A few simple habits keep multi-device setups safe:

- Give each person their own account where possible, rather than sharing one. Shared accounts make it easy for one person to delete something another person needed.
- Use shared albums or folders for sharing, instead of signing everyone in to the same account. Most photo and cloud services support sharing without handing over full access.
- Make sure at least one backup is independent of sync, so a deletion on any device can be undone from the backup.
- Check what each device is syncing. An old tablet in a drawer can still be signed in, still syncing, and still able to change what's in your account.

## Test your setup

An untested backup might not work. A few minutes of checking tells you whether it does.

- Check the dates. Open each backup, including photos, device backup and chat backups, and look at when it last completed. If any are weeks old, find out why.
- Check the storage. Make sure your cloud accounts aren't full. A full account stops backing up.
- Check the contents. Look for a few specific photos and contacts in your backup, including older ones.
- Try a small restore. Restore a single photo or file from your backup onto your phone. If it works, you know the backup is real.

Do this every few months, and always before a big change such as a software update, a repair or a new phone. Our guide to [backing up before switching phones](/daily-info/backup-before-switching-phones) covers that last one in detail.

## An example

Say you're clearing space on a full phone. You select a few hundred old photos and delete them, confident they're "in the cloud".

If your photos are only synced, those photos move to the trash in your photo service and, after the trash period, disappear from the cloud too. If you notice in time, you can restore them from the trash. If you don't, they're gone.

If your photos are also backed up somewhere that keeps its own copy, deleting them from the phone doesn't touch that copy. They stay safe in your backup, and you can restore any of them whenever you like, months or years later.

The phone, the photos and the mistake are the same in both cases. The difference is whether a backup sat behind the sync.
