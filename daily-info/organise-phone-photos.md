A phone camera roll can grow by hundreds of photos a month: pictures of family and friends, screenshots, receipts, things you wanted to remember, photos of the same sunset taken twelve times. After a few years, it's tens of thousands of pictures, most of which you'll never look at again, and the ones you care about are buried somewhere in the middle.

Organising a huge photo library doesn't mean sorting every picture by hand. It means clearing the clutter that makes it hard to find anything, setting up a few simple structures you'll keep using, and letting your phone do the searching. This guide walks through it step by step, in an order that makes quick progress early and never puts your photos at risk.

The steps work on both Android phones and iPhones. Where features differ, both are mentioned.

## Back up your photos first

Before you delete, move or reorganise anything, make sure your photos and videos are backed up. Organising involves a lot of deleting, and it's easy to remove something you later wish you'd kept.

Back up to cloud storage or a computer, and confirm the backup has actually finished. A large library can take a long time to upload for the first time, and backups can pause without warning when the phone is on mobile data, low on battery or out of cloud storage. Look for a clear sign in your backup app that everything is uploaded, and spot-check a few older photos.

Also check what happens to your backup when you delete a photo from your phone. Some services keep their own copy; others sync the deletion, so the photo disappears from the cloud too. If yours syncs deletions, be extra careful during the clear-out steps below, or make an additional copy on a computer first. Our guide to [backup vs sync]({{ '/daily-info/backup-vs-sync' | relative_url }}) explains the difference.

## Set a realistic goal

Photo clear-outs usually fail because they try to do everything at once. Sitting down to "sort out all my photos" with 30,000 pictures is overwhelming, and it's easy to give up after half an hour.

Instead, pick a goal you can finish:

- By type, such as "clear all my screenshots" or "delete duplicates".
- By time, such as "sort out this year" or "go through last month".
- By size, such as "free up 10 GB" by working through videos.

Each small goal gives you a visible result, which makes it much easier to keep going. A good approach is to start with the current year and work backwards, since recent photos are the ones you look at most, and older years can wait.

## Clear out screenshots

Screenshots are the easiest win in almost any camera roll. They pile up constantly, including tickets, directions, recipes, messages and things you meant to look at later, and very few are worth keeping long term.

Most phones group screenshots automatically. On an iPhone, look for the **Screenshots** album under Media Types in the Photos app. On Android, most gallery apps and Google Photos have a **Screenshots** album or folder.

Open it, scroll through, and select everything you no longer need. It's common to clear hundreds of screenshots in a few minutes. If a screenshot holds something you need, like a code, a confirmation or an important message, consider saving that information somewhere more appropriate, such as a note, and then delete the screenshot.

## Remove duplicates and bursts

The next easy target is repeated photos: the same picture saved twice, or ten near-identical attempts at the same shot.

Exact duplicates can appear when photos are imported more than once, or saved from a chat after you'd already taken them. On an iPhone, the Photos app finds exact duplicates for you in the **Duplicates** album under Utilities, and can merge them, keeping the best-quality version. Some Android gallery apps offer similar suggestions for similar or duplicate photos.

Burst photos are the rapid sequences taken when you hold the shutter button. On an iPhone, a burst appears as one stack; open it and choose **Select** to keep your favourites and discard the rest. On Android, bursts are often grouped in the gallery in a similar way, depending on your phone.

Near-duplicates are the hardest, since only you can decide which of five similar group photos is the best. A quick rule helps: keep the one where everyone's eyes are open and the focus is sharp, and delete the rest without second-guessing.

## Review your videos

Videos deserve their own pass. They take up far more space than photos, a single long clip can use as much storage as hundreds of pictures, and they're often forgotten because you rarely scroll through them.

Both the iPhone Photos app and most Android gallery apps let you view only your videos. Go through them looking for:

- Accidental recordings, like the few seconds of floor filmed while you were trying to take a photo.
- Long clips you only needed a moment of. Most phones let you trim a video, keeping just the part you want. Check whether trimming saves a new copy or replaces the original, and keep the original in your backup if you're unsure.
- Duplicates of the same moment filmed several times.
- Videos you've already shared and don't need to keep on the phone, such as clips sent on to someone else.

Clearing even a handful of long videos can make a noticeable difference to both your storage and how easy your library is to browse.

## Delete the obvious mistakes

Every camera roll has photos that were never meant to be taken: pictures of the floor, the inside of a pocket, a blurry thumb, a black screen. Some phones can suggest these automatically, but they're also easy to spot while scrolling quickly.

Set yourself a simple rule while scrolling: if you can tell at a glance that a photo is a mistake, delete it immediately. Don't stop to think about photos that might be worth keeping; skip them and come back later. Speed matters more than perfection at this stage.

Remember that deleted photos usually go to a **Recently Deleted** or **Trash** folder for a while before being removed permanently. That's a helpful safety net during a big clear-out. Leave it alone until you're finished, then check it before emptying it. Our guide to [where deleted photos go on Android]({{ '/daily-info/where-deleted-photos-go-android' | relative_url }}) explains how long different apps keep them.

## Sort out chat app photos

Chat apps can save every photo, video, meme and sticker you're sent into your camera roll or a separate folder. In busy group chats, that can be thousands of images you never chose to keep.

First, stop the flow. Most chat apps let you turn off automatically saving media to your gallery, or limit automatic downloads to certain file types. In WhatsApp, for example, this is in the app's settings under chats and storage options.

Then deal with what's already there. Many gallery apps show chat app pictures as their own album or folder. Scroll through it, keep the photos that matter to you, and delete the rest. Since the photos usually also exist inside the chat itself, removing them from your gallery doesn't remove them from the conversation.

## Deal with photos of documents

Phones have become the place we photograph everything we need to remember: receipts, parking spots, whiteboards, forms, labels, prescriptions and ID. These photos are useful for a while, then become clutter, but a few of them are important to keep.

Sort them into three groups:

- Short-term reminders, like parking spots, shop labels or a menu. Delete these once they've done their job.
- Things to keep for a while, like receipts for returns or warranty claims. Move these into a Documents album, or save them as files, and delete them when they're no longer needed.
- Important records, like ID, insurance details or official letters. Keep these somewhere secure rather than in your camera roll, where anyone scrolling your photos could see them. A locked or hidden folder, or a secure document store, is more appropriate.

Many phones can now recognise text in photos, so you can find a receipt by searching for the shop's name. That makes it easier to clear the rest without worrying about losing the one you need.

## Use favourites for the best photos

Once the clutter is gone, the next step is making your best photos easy to find. The simplest tool for this is the favourite button.

Both the iPhone Photos app and Google Photos let you mark any photo as a favourite with a single tap, usually a heart or a star. Favourites are collected in their own album, which becomes a highlights reel of the photos you care about most.

Make it a habit to favourite great photos as you take them, and during your clear-outs. It takes a second per photo, and it means that when someone asks to see a picture from your holiday, you're not scrolling through three hundred to find it.

## Make a few albums that last

Albums are useful, but only if you keep them up to date. The most common mistake is creating dozens of very specific albums, then abandoning them after a few weeks.

A better approach is a small number of broad albums that stay useful for years. Good candidates include:

- People you photograph often, like partners, children, parents or pets. (Your phone may already group faces automatically; see the next step.)
- Trips and events, one album per holiday, wedding or big occasion.
- Documents, for photos of receipts, warranties, ID and paperwork you need to keep. Consider also saving these as files somewhere secure.
- To print or share, for photos you mean to do something with.

Aim for albums you'll add to naturally. If an album hasn't been touched in a year, it probably doesn't need to exist.

## Let search do the work

Modern phones are good at finding photos without any organising at all. Both the iPhone Photos app and Google Photos can search by:

- What's in the picture, such as "beach", "dog", "cake" or "car".
- Where it was taken, using the location stored in the photo, if location was turned on.
- When it was taken, by month, year or season.
- Who is in it, if you've set up face grouping and named people.
- Text in the photo, such as words on a sign or receipt, on many recent phones.

This means you don't need an album for every occasion. If you want a photo of your dog on the beach last summer, searching is usually faster than any album structure you could build by hand.

To get the most out of search, take a few minutes to review the **People** section, where your phone groups faces, and add names to the people you photograph most. Face grouping is processed and stored differently by each service, so check the settings if you'd prefer it turned off.

## Share without cluttering your library

Sharing photos adds a lot of clutter, to your own library and to everyone else's. A few habits keep it under control.

- Use shared albums for events. Instead of sending dozens of photos from a party to everyone individually, create a shared album and invite people to view or add their own. Everyone gets the photos, without flooding chats or saving copies to every phone.
- Choose what you save from others. When friends share photos with you, save only the ones you want, rather than all of them.
- Share links for big batches. Many photo services let you share a link to a selection of photos, which is tidier than sending them one by one.

## Hide private photos

Some photos don't belong in a library you might scroll through in front of other people: medical photos, documents, or private moments. Rather than deleting them, you can move them somewhere more discreet. It's also worth checking [which apps can see your photos]({{ '/daily-info/check-app-permissions-photos' | relative_url }}) at the same time.

- On an iPhone, you can hide a photo from the main library. It moves to the **Hidden** album, which can be locked so it needs Face ID, Touch ID or your passcode to open.
- In Google Photos, the **Locked Folder** keeps photos behind your screen lock and out of your main library and search. Be aware that photos in the Locked Folder aren't included in your backup by default, so don't use it as the only home for photos you can't afford to lose.
- Many Android phone makers also offer a hidden album or a secure folder in their gallery or settings.

## Work through older years gradually

Once your recent photos are tidy, you may want to tackle older years. There's no rush, and the best way to do it is gradually, one year at a time.

The memories features on your phone help here. Both the iPhone Photos app and Google Photos regularly suggest photos from past years, such as "on this day" collections. When a memory appears, take a minute to delete the duplicates and mistakes in it, and favourite the best photos. Over months, your older years get tidied without ever setting aside a big block of time.

If you'd rather do it in one go, set aside half an hour per year of photos, starting with the most recent year you haven't sorted. Clear screenshots, duplicates and mistakes first, as in the steps above, and only then decide on albums.

## Build a monthly habit

A tidy photo library comes from five minutes once a month more than from one big clear-out a year.

Set a reminder for the first weekend of each month, and do this:

1. Delete the month's screenshots you no longer need.
2. Clear bursts and near-duplicates from anything you took lots of photos of.
3. Favourite the best photos of the month.
4. Add photos to your long-term albums, like trips or people.
5. Check your backup is still running and up to date.

Done regularly, it takes minutes, and your camera roll stays manageable however many photos you take.

## Keep your library safe as it grows

A well-organised library is only useful while it exists, and it becomes more valuable as it grows.

Keep your backup running all the time rather than when you remember. Check it every few months by looking at the date of the last backup and spot-checking a few photos. Keep an eye on your cloud storage, since a full account stops backing up. And for the photos you'd be most devastated to lose, consider keeping a second copy on a computer or external drive too.
