Switching between Android and iPhone used to mean accepting that some things would be left behind. Photos were often the biggest casualty: albums scattered, videos missing, pictures arriving as blurry, compressed copies. Things are much better now. Both Apple and Google provide tools for moving to their phones, cloud photo services work on both platforms, and there are several reliable ways to get your library across in full quality.

This guide covers the practical options for moving photos and videos from Android to iPhone, and from iPhone to Android, what to check before you start, and how to make sure everything arrived before you say goodbye to the old phone.

## Before you start

A little preparation makes any transfer smoother, whichever direction you're going and whichever method you use.

**Back up your photos first.** Whatever method you choose, make sure your photos and videos are also backed up somewhere safe, such as cloud storage or a computer. Transfers occasionally stop partway, and a backup means nothing depends on the transfer going perfectly.

**Check the space on the new phone.** Look at how much space your photos and videos take on the old phone (in Settings, under Storage) and make sure the new phone has more than that free. If the new phone has less storage than the old one, you'll need to choose what to move, or keep part of your library in the cloud.

**Make room if you need to.** If the old phone is nearly full, our guide to [freeing up storage without losing anything](/daily-info/free-up-phone-storage) covers the safest ways to clear space first.

**Tidy up.** Moving fewer photos is quicker and leaves you with a tidier library. Delete screenshots you don't need, duplicates and obvious mistakes before you start. Our guide on [organising thousands of photos](/daily-info/organise-phone-photos) has quick ways to do this.

**Charge both phones and use Wi-Fi.** Large libraries take a while to move, and some methods use a lot of data. Plug both phones in and keep them on a good Wi-Fi connection.

**Allow time.** A few hundred photos can move in minutes; tens of thousands of photos and hours of video can take much longer. Don't start a big transfer an hour before you need to leave the house.

## Understanding photo formats

Android phones and iPhones don't always save photos and videos in the same formats, and this explains most of the "why won't this open?" moments after a switch.

**Android phones** usually save photos as **JPEG** files, which open almost everywhere. Some newer Android phones can also save in HEIF or other efficient formats if you choose.

**iPhones** save photos as **HEIC** files by default, and videos in **HEVC** (also called H.265). These formats keep quality high while using less space. Most recent Android phones can open them, but some older phones, computers and apps can't.

If you're moving from iPhone to Android and are worried about compatibility, there are two useful settings on the iPhone:

- In **Settings, then Camera, then Formats**, choosing **Most Compatible** makes the iPhone save new photos as JPEG and new videos as H.264. This only affects photos taken from then on.
- In **Settings, then Photos**, the **Transfer to Mac or PC** option set to **Automatic** converts photos to a compatible format when they're copied to a computer.

**Live Photos** are another iPhone feature worth knowing about. They're a still photo plus a short clip. When moved to Android, they usually arrive as a still photo, sometimes with a separate short video file.

## Which method should you use?

There's no single best way to move photos between Android and iPhone. The right choice depends on whether the new phone is already set up, how big your library is, and whether you have a computer or cloud storage available.

| Method | Best when | Things to know |
|---|---|---|
| Move to iOS (Android to iPhone) | You're setting up a new iPhone | Only works during the iPhone's initial setup |
| Android setup tools (iPhone to Android) | You're setting up a new Android phone | Options vary by phone maker |
| Cloud photo service | The new phone is already set up, or you want a backup too | Needs enough cloud storage and time to upload |
| Computer | Very large libraries, or slow internet | Takes more steps, but leaves a full copy on the computer |
| Phone-to-phone transfer app | Both phones are in the same room | Keep both phones charged and nearby |

For most people setting up a brand-new phone, the phone's own setup tool is the easiest starting point. If you've already set up the new phone, or the setup transfer missed something, a cloud service, a computer or a transfer app can fill the gaps afterwards.

## Android to iPhone: use Move to iOS

Apple makes a free Android app called **Move to iOS**, available from Google Play. It's designed for exactly this job and is usually the simplest option.

It works during the iPhone's initial setup. When the new iPhone asks how you'd like to transfer your apps and data, choose the option to move data from Android. The iPhone shows a code, which you enter in the Move to iOS app on your Android phone. The two phones then connect directly over a temporary private Wi-Fi network created by the iPhone, and you choose what to transfer.

As well as photos and videos, Move to iOS can transfer things like contacts, message history, calendars, email accounts, web bookmarks and more. Photo albums are usually brought across as well.

A few tips for a smooth transfer:

- **Keep both phones plugged in** and close together until the transfer finishes.
- **Don't use the Android phone during the transfer.** Leave the Move to iOS app open on screen, and avoid taking calls if you can.
- **Turn off anything that might switch the Android phone's Wi-Fi network**, such as a setting that automatically moves to mobile data when Wi-Fi is weak.
- **Be patient with large libraries.** The iPhone may show an estimated time; with lots of videos it can be long.

The important limitation is timing: Move to iOS only works during setup. If your iPhone is already set up, you'd need to erase it and start again to use it, or choose one of the other methods below.

## iPhone to Android: use the new phone's setup tools

Moving the other way, Android phones have their own built-in help. During setup, most Android phones offer to **copy apps and data** from your old phone, including from an iPhone.

The details vary by phone maker. Many phones let you connect the iPhone and the new Android phone with a cable (using an adapter if needed) to copy photos, videos, contacts and more directly. Some manufacturers also provide their own switching apps, such as Samsung's Smart Switch, which can transfer from an iPhone by cable or by importing from iCloud. Google also offers a **Switch to Android** app for iPhone, which guides you through moving photos, videos, contacts and calendars.

Before you switch, there's one iPhone-specific step that's easy to forget: **turn off iMessage**. If you don't, messages from other iPhone users may keep going to iMessage rather than arriving as texts on your new Android phone. Turn it off in Settings, under Messages, before you move your SIM. If you no longer have the iPhone, Apple provides a way to deregister your phone number from iMessage online.

## Using a cloud photo service

Cloud photo services work on both Android and iPhone, and they're a flexible way to move a library, particularly if you want to take your time or have already set up the new phone.

The general approach is the same for any service:

1. **Install the service on the old phone** and back up your photos and videos to it, making sure to choose original or full quality if the service offers a choice.
2. **Wait for the upload to finish completely.** Check inside the app that everything has been backed up.
3. **Install the same service on the new phone** and sign in with the same account. Your photos appear in the app.
4. **Download them to the new phone** if you want them stored on the device and in its own gallery, rather than just viewable in the app.

**Google Photos** is available on both platforms, so it's a common choice for moving in either direction. **iCloud Photos** is built into iPhones, and can be viewed on Android through a web browser, which makes it better for getting photos onto an iPhone than off it.

Keep an eye on storage limits. Free cloud storage allowances are limited (Google accounts include 15 GB shared across Gmail, Drive and Photos; iCloud includes 5 GB), so a large library may need a paid plan, even if just for a month while you switch.

## Using a computer

If you have a computer, it offers a reliable, cable-based route that doesn't depend on your internet speed or cloud storage.

**From Android to a computer:** connect the phone with a USB cable and, when asked on the phone, choose to transfer files. On a Windows PC, the phone appears as a device, and your camera photos are usually in a folder called DCIM. A Mac doesn't show Android phones in Finder by default, so you'll need a file transfer app that supports Android. Copy the photos and videos to a folder on the computer.

**From the computer to an iPhone:** on a Mac, you can sync photos from a folder to the iPhone using Finder, or import them into the Photos app and use iCloud Photos. On Windows, Apple's software for iPhone can sync a folder of photos to the device.

**From iPhone to a computer and then Android:** connect the iPhone and import its photos using the computer's photo tools, then copy them onto the Android phone by USB.

This route takes a little more effort, but it has a big advantage: you end up with a complete copy of your library on the computer, which doubles as a backup.

## Using a phone-to-phone transfer app

Transfer apps move photos, videos and files directly between two phones, usually over a local wireless connection, without uploading everything to the internet first. They're useful when both phones are in the same room, especially for large video files, and when you've already set up the new phone.

As with any method, keep both phones charged and nearby, choose what to send before you start, and check everything arrived before deleting anything from the old phone. ## Why not just use chat or email?

Sending photos through a chat app or email is fine for a handful of pictures, but it's not a good way to move a library.

- **Chat apps often compress photos and videos**, so what arrives is a smaller, lower-quality copy. Some chat apps have a "send as document" or "original quality" option that avoids this, but it's easy to forget.
- **Email has size limits**, which rules out most videos and large batches of photos.
- **Details can be lost.** Information stored inside a photo, such as the date and place it was taken, can be stripped when it's sent through some apps, which leaves photos out of order in your new gallery.

Keep chat and email for sharing a few pictures, and use one of the methods above for the real move.

## What doesn't move easily

Even with the best tools, a few things don't cross between platforms neatly. Knowing about them in advance saves surprises.

- **Edits.** Photos edited in one phone's gallery app may arrive as the edited version only, or as the original only, depending on the method.
- **Albums.** Some methods keep albums; others bring everything across as one collection, or turn albums into folders.
- **Favourites and hidden photos.** Marking photos as favourites, and hidden or locked folders, often don't carry over. Check hidden and locked folders on the old phone, as their contents may not be included in a transfer at all.
- **Shared albums.** Albums shared with other people through a particular service stay with that service.
- **Photos inside other apps.** Pictures saved inside chat apps, notes apps or social media apps may not be part of your main photo library, and need moving separately or saving to the gallery first.

## Common problems and how to fix them

Most transfer problems have simple causes. If something goes wrong, work through these before trying anything drastic.

**The transfer stops partway through.** This is usually caused by one phone going to sleep, losing Wi-Fi or running low on battery. Plug both phones in, keep them close together, and start again. Most tools skip what's already been copied, or you can move the remaining photos with a different method.

**The new phone runs out of space.** If the transfer fails because the new phone is full, you'll need to move less. Leave older photos and long videos in the cloud or on a computer, and move only what you want on the phone itself.

**Photos appear in the wrong order.** Galleries sort by the date a photo was taken, which is stored inside the photo file. If some photos appear under the date of the transfer instead, the date information was lost along the way, which often happens with photos that were sent through chat apps. Moving them with a method that copies original files usually fixes it.

**Videos won't play.** The video may be in a format the new phone's gallery doesn't support, or the file may not have finished copying. Try opening it in a different app, and check the file size matches the original.

**Photos look blurry or small.** They've probably been compressed by the method used to move them, typically a chat app, or a cloud service set to a reduced-quality option. Move the originals again using a method that keeps full quality.

**Some photos are missing entirely.** Check whether they were in a hidden or locked folder, saved inside another app, or stored on a memory card rather than the phone. These are often skipped by transfer tools.

## Check everything arrived

Before you reset the old phone, hand it on or trade it in, take ten minutes to confirm the move worked.

- **Compare the numbers.** Look at how many photos and videos the old phone shows, and compare with the new phone. They won't always match exactly (Live Photos and edits can change the count), but they should be close.
- **Scroll back through time.** Check a few photos from different years, including your oldest ones.
- **Play some videos**, particularly long ones, to make sure they arrived complete.
- **Check the dates.** Photos should appear in the right order. If a batch appears all on one day, the date information may not have come across.
- **Look for the folders people forget**: screenshots, downloads, and pictures saved from chat apps.

If anything's missing, the old phone still has it. Move the missing items across, then check again.

## Keep the old phone for a while

The final piece of advice is the simplest: don't rush to reset the old phone. Keep it, untouched, for a week or two while you use the new one. It's surprisingly common to discover a missing album or a folder of videos several days after switching, and with the old phone still intact, fixing it takes minutes.
