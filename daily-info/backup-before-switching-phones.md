Switching phones is one of the easiest times to lose something you didn't mean to: a photo album, a saved contact, a chat history, a document you were partway through, or the codes you use to sign in to your email and bank. The new phone arrives, the setup screens move quickly, and it's tempting to assume everything will follow you across. Much of it will. The trouble is the part that doesn't, and you usually only notice once the old phone has been wiped or handed on.

A little preparation beforehand makes the move uneventful instead of stressful. This checklist walks through it in the order that works best: first work out what's on the old phone, then back up each type of data properly, then deal with the things that are easy to forget (sign-in codes, your SIM, the accounts tied to the old device), and only then reset the old phone and restore everything onto the new one.

You don't need to do it all in one sitting. Starting a few days before the new phone arrives gives large photo backups time to finish, and gives you time to check each step properly rather than rushing it on the day. Each step below is short, and most take only a few minutes once you know where to look.

## Take stock of what's on your old phone

Before you back anything up, spend ten minutes working out what you need to keep. It's much easier to make a list now than to try to remember later what used to be on a phone you no longer have.

Go through your home screens and app list, and note down anything that holds information you care about. The list usually looks something like this:

- Photos and videos, including screenshots, downloads and pictures saved from chat apps
- Contacts, especially any that were added years ago and may be stored only on the phone
- Messages: text messages and chat apps such as WhatsApp or Signal
- Two-factor codes from an authenticator app, and any saved passwords
- App data: notes, documents, game progress, fitness history and anything else kept inside an app
- Files such as PDFs, tickets, boarding passes and voice recordings
- Settings you'd rather not set up again, like Wi-Fi passwords and home screen layout

While you're going through, it's also worth deleting what you don't need. Duplicate photos, blurry shots, old downloads and apps you haven't opened in a year all make the backup larger and slower for no benefit. A smaller backup finishes sooner and is easier to check.

Keep the list somewhere other than the phone itself, whether that's a note on another device, an email to yourself or a piece of paper. You'll use it again at the end to confirm everything made it across.

## Back up your photos and videos

Your camera roll is usually the largest and most irreplaceable thing on your phone. Before you do anything else, confirm it's backed up somewhere other than the phone itself, such as a cloud storage account. A memory card can be lost in the same move.

### Use a separate backup, not a second copy on the phone

A memory card or a folder on the same phone isn't a backup in the way that matters: if the phone is lost, stolen or reset, the copy goes with it. A backup should live somewhere separate. Cloud storage is the most convenient option because it works over Wi-Fi and doesn't depend on you remembering to plug anything in. Copying everything to a computer is a reasonable second copy if you have one.

### Check that the backup actually finished

This is the step people skip. Large photo libraries can take hours or even days to upload the first time, and a backup can pause without warning when the phone is on mobile data, low on battery or out of cloud storage. Open your backup app and look for a clear sign that everything is uploaded, rather than assuming it is. Then check a few photos from different years on another device or in a web browser, to confirm they're there.

### Watch for folders that aren't included by default

Many backup apps only include the main camera folder unless you tell them otherwise. Pictures saved from chat apps, screenshots and downloaded images often live in separate folders. If they matter to you, check they're included. It's also worth checking whether your backup keeps photos at full quality or saves smaller, compressed copies, so there are no surprises later.

## Make sure your contacts are synced to an account

Contacts are easy to overlook because they usually sync in the background. Check rather than assume: where does each contact live?

Contacts can be stored in three places: in an online account (such as the Google account on an Android phone or iCloud on an iPhone), on the phone itself, or on the SIM card. Contacts saved to an account follow you automatically to any new phone signed in to the same account. Contacts saved only to the phone or SIM don't, and are the ones that get lost.

To check, open your contacts app and look at its settings. Most contacts apps let you see which account each contact belongs to, or show a count of contacts per account. If some are stored on the phone or SIM, most apps offer an option to move or copy them to your account. Look in your phone's account settings for a contacts sync option, and confirm the last sync date isn't stale.

As an extra safety net, most contacts apps can also export everything as a single file (usually called a vCard or .vcf file). Save that file somewhere off the phone, such as your cloud storage or an email to yourself. If anything goes wrong with the sync, you can import that file on the new phone in a few taps.

Do the same quick check for your calendar. Events usually sync to the same account as your contacts, but a calendar kept only on the device won't move by itself.

## Save your messages and chat history

Messages are often the thing people are most upset to lose, and they're also one of the least predictable parts of a phone switch, because text messages and each chat app handle backups differently.

### Text messages

On an Android phone, text messages are usually included in the phone's built-in Google backup, which you can check in the backup section of Settings. On an iPhone, messages can be kept in iCloud. If you're staying with the same type of phone, restoring that backup on the new phone brings your messages back. If you're switching between Android and iPhone, the new phone's setup tool can often bring text messages across, but it's worth checking what it lists before you rely on it.

### Chat apps

Apps like WhatsApp, Signal and Telegram each manage their own history, separately from your phone's backup. WhatsApp, for example, has its own chat backup in its settings and its own process for moving chats between Android and iPhone. Signal keeps messages only on your devices and uses its own transfer when you set up a new phone. Open each chat app you use, find its backup or transfer option, and follow it before the old phone is reset.

Timing matters here. Some chat apps only offer to restore your history when you first install and verify the app on the new phone. If you skip that screen, getting the history back later can be difficult, so set up your chat apps carefully and read each prompt.

## Check app data as well as photos

Some apps keep data locally on the device instead of in the cloud: notes, saved logins, offline documents, drafts, recordings. If an app matters to you, check its own settings for an export or backup option before you set the old phone aside.

A good way to do this is to go through your list from the first step, app by app, and ask one question: if I installed this app fresh on a new phone and signed in, would everything still be there? For apps where you have an account and your data syncs online, the answer is usually yes. For apps without an account, often it isn't.

A few categories are worth a closer look:

- Notes and to-do apps. Some sync to an account, some only store notes on the device. If there's no account, look for an export option.
- Games. Progress is often tied to a games account or the game's own login. If a game has never asked you to sign in, its progress may only exist on this phone.
- Fitness and health data. Step counts, workouts and health records may sync to an account, or may need a separate export.
- Documents and downloads. Files saved inside an app, like offline maps, downloaded PDFs or tickets, may need to be moved or downloaded again.
- Voice recordings and scans. These are easy to forget and are often stored only on the device.

For any app that doesn't sync, export what you need or copy it to your cloud storage. It takes a few minutes.

## Move your two-factor codes and passwords

This is the step with the most serious consequences if it's missed. Many people use an authenticator app to generate the six-digit codes that protect their email, banking and social media accounts. Those codes are tied to the app on your old phone. If you reset the old phone before moving them, you can find yourself locked out of important accounts, and recovering them can take days.

Start by opening your authenticator app and checking whether it has a transfer, export or cloud sync option. Many authenticator apps now offer one, but not all do, and some only sync if you turned it on earlier. If yours has a transfer option, use it to move your accounts to the new phone, then test a code from each account before going any further.

If your authenticator app doesn't offer a transfer, you'll need to move each account individually: sign in to the account on a computer, open its security settings, and set up two-factor authentication again with the new phone. While you're there, save the backup codes each service offers and keep them somewhere safe off the phone. Those codes are your way back in if something goes wrong.

Codes sent by text message are simpler: they follow your phone number, so they'll work on the new phone as soon as your SIM or eSIM is active.

Passwords saved in your browser or a password manager usually sync through your account, but check. Open your password manager on another device, or on the web, and confirm your passwords are there before you rely on it.

## Sort out your SIM or eSIM

Your phone number lives on your SIM, and more and more phones now use an eSIM, which is built into the phone rather than being a card you can move by hand.

If you have a physical SIM card, moving it is usually as simple as switching it into the new phone. Check that the new phone takes the same size of SIM first. If you have an eSIM, the transfer happens through your mobile provider or the phone's setup screens, and the method varies between providers and phone makers. Some let you transfer an eSIM directly from the old phone during setup; others need you to request a new eSIM from your provider.

Whichever you have, sort out your number before you reset the old phone. Many security checks, including bank logins and account recovery, send a code by text message, and you'll want to be able to receive those codes throughout the switch. Keep the old phone working until the new phone can make calls, send texts and receive a test code.

If you're also changing providers at the same time, do that as a separate step. Moving your number and your data on the same day doubles the number of things that can go wrong at once.

## Sign out and unlock the old phone

Modern phones have anti-theft protection built in. It's a good thing, but it can cause problems when a phone changes hands. If you reset an Android phone without removing your Google account first, the next person to set it up may be asked for your account details before they can use it. An iPhone that still has Find My turned on stays locked to your Apple account with Activation Lock, even after it's reset.

So before you reset, sign out properly:

- On Android, remove your Google account (and any other accounts) from the Accounts section of Settings.
- On iPhone, turn off Find My and sign out of your Apple account in Settings.
- Unpair any smartwatch or other accessories that are linked to the old phone.
- Remove the old phone from your trusted devices in any banking or security app that lets you register devices.

If you're moving from an iPhone to an Android phone, there's one more thing: turn off iMessage before you switch, so messages from iPhone users arrive as normal texts on your new phone rather than going to an iPhone you no longer use. Apple also provides a way to deregister your number from iMessage if you no longer have the old phone.

Signing out also protects you. It means the old phone no longer has access to your email, photos or accounts, whoever ends up with it.

## Back up before you factory reset, not after

If you plan to wipe the old phone before trading it in or handing it down, always confirm the new phone has everything it needs *first*. Once a factory reset runs, anything that wasn't backed up is gone.

Get your list out from the first step and go through it one line at a time, on the new phone:

- Open your photos and scroll back through a few different years.
- Search for a handful of contacts, including some old ones.
- Open your chat apps and check your conversations are there.
- Open your authenticator app and confirm each code works.
- Open the apps that hold your notes, documents and other data.

Only when every item is ticked off should you reset the old phone. Before you do, remove the SIM card and any memory card. The memory card may hold photos that aren't anywhere else, and a SIM card left in a phone you're giving away is an easy way to hand someone access to your number.

There's no rush. Keep the old phone, fully intact, for a week or two after switching. If you notice something missing in that time, the old phone still has it.

## Restore in the same order

When you set up the new phone, restore photos and contacts first, then reinstall the apps you use most. That way, if anything looks off, you'll notice it early rather than weeks later when the old phone is already gone.

Set up with the new phone plugged in and connected to Wi-Fi. Restoring can use a lot of data and battery, and running out of either partway through causes problems that are easy to avoid.

Pay attention during the first setup screens. On both Android and iPhone, the option to restore from a backup, or to copy data directly from your old phone, appears during initial setup. If you skip it, getting back to it usually means resetting the new phone and starting again. When you're asked, sign in to the same account you used on the old phone and choose to restore.

Once the basics are in place, work through the rest in this order:

1. Your main account and cloud storage, so your photos, contacts and calendars start syncing.
2. Chat apps, restoring each one's history when it asks during setup.
3. Your authenticator app, testing a code for each account.
4. Banking and other security-sensitive apps, which may ask you to verify the new phone.
5. Everything else, starting with the apps you use daily.

Finally, check that your backup is running again on the new phone. It's easy to restore everything and then forget to turn backups back on. That backup is what protects you the next time you switch phones, or if this one is lost or damaged.
