A full phone gets in the way quickly. The camera refuses to take another picture. An update won't install. Apps slow down or crash. The warning often arrives halfway through filming something you wanted to keep.

The usual reaction is to start deleting whatever looks biggest, as fast as possible. That frees space, but it's also exactly how people lose photos and files they meant to keep. This guide takes a calmer approach: find out what's filling your phone, make sure anything that matters is safely backed up, and then clear space in the order that wins back the most room with the least risk.

Most of the steps work on both Android phones and iPhones. Menu names vary between phone makers and software versions, so where the exact wording differs, look for the closest match in your own settings.

## Find out what's using your storage

Before deleting anything, look at where your space has gone. It's rarely what you expect, and a two-minute check stops you deleting things that barely matter while the real culprit carries on growing.

On an iPhone, open Settings, then General, then iPhone Storage. After a moment, you'll see a coloured bar showing how your storage is divided, followed by a list of apps sorted by how much space each one uses.

On an Android phone, open Settings and look for Storage (on some phones it sits under Battery and device care, or Device care). You'll see storage split into categories such as images, videos, audio, documents, apps and system, and you can tap each one to see more detail.

Look for the two or three biggest items. It's usually one or more of these:

- Photos and videos, usually the largest category by far
- Chat apps, which save every photo, video and voice note you're sent
- Streaming and music apps, holding downloaded episodes, films, songs and podcasts
- Games, which can be several gigabytes each
- System, the space used by the operating system itself, which you can't do much about

Make a note of what you find. It tells you which of the steps below will make the most difference, and which you can skip.

## Back up before you delete anything

This is the step that separates freeing up space from losing things. Before you delete any photos, videos or files, make sure they exist somewhere other than your phone.

Start with your photos and videos, since they're both the biggest category and the least replaceable. Back them up to cloud storage, or copy them to a computer, and then check that the backup actually finished. Large libraries can take hours to upload the first time, and a backup can pause without warning when the phone is on mobile data, low on battery or out of cloud space.

To check, open your backup app and look for a clear sign that everything has been uploaded. Then pick a few photos from different months and years and confirm they're there, ideally by looking on another device or in a web browser.

There's one more thing to check: what happens to your backup when you delete a photo from the phone. Some services keep a separate copy that stays put, which is what you want when freeing space. Others sync deletions, so removing a photo from the phone removes it from the cloud as well. If you're not sure which yours does, our guide to [backup vs sync]({{ '/daily-info/backup-vs-sync' | relative_url }}) explains the difference and how to tell.

Only when you're confident that your photos and videos are safe should you start deleting them from the phone.

## Clear out photos and videos you don't need

Once your library is backed up, the fastest way to free space is usually to remove the photos and videos you don't want. You don't need to go through years of pictures one by one. A few targeted sweeps make a big difference.

Start with videos. Videos use far more space than photos. A single minute of high-quality video can take up hundreds of megabytes, and 4K video even more. Most photo apps let you view just your videos. Look through them for long clips you don't need, accidental recordings and duplicates.

Then screenshots. Screenshots pile up fast: tickets, directions, recipes, messages, things you meant to look at later. Most are no longer needed after a day or two. Many gallery apps group screenshots into their own album, which makes them easy to review and clear in one go.

Then duplicates and bursts. Burst mode and repeated attempts at the same shot can leave dozens of near-identical photos. Keep the best one or two and delete the rest. Recent iPhones can find exact duplicates for you in the Photos app, under the Duplicates album. Some Android gallery apps offer similar suggestions.

Then the obvious mistakes. Blurry shots, pictures of your pocket, photos of the floor. They take a moment to spot and are always safe to remove.

For more ideas, our guide on [organising thousands of photos on your phone]({{ '/daily-info/organise-phone-photos' | relative_url }}) goes through a full clear-out step by step.

## Empty the trash, but only when you're sure

This part catches a lot of people out. On most phones, deleting a photo doesn't free its space straight away. It moves to a **Recently Deleted**, **Trash** or **Bin** folder, where it stays for a set time, often 30 days, before it's removed for good. Until then, it still takes up room.

That safety net is valuable, and it's the reason a deleted photo can often be recovered. So don't empty the trash as a reflex. First, scroll through it and make sure there's nothing in there you want back. If you've just done a big clear-out, it can be worth waiting a day or two, in case you realise you deleted something by mistake.

When you're sure, empty the trash to reclaim the space. On an iPhone, open the Photos app and find Recently Deleted, which is usually near the bottom of your albums. On Android, check the trash or bin in whichever gallery app you use, and in your file manager if you delete files there too. Our guide to [where deleted photos go on Android]({{ '/daily-info/where-deleted-photos-go-android' | relative_url }}) lists the most common places.

## Deal with chat app media

Chat apps are often the hidden cause of a full phone. Every photo, video, voice note, sticker and document you're sent can be saved to the phone automatically, including the ones from busy group chats you barely read.

Most major chat apps have a storage tool that shows exactly how much space each conversation is using, and lets you clear large files without deleting the messages themselves. In WhatsApp, for example, it's under Settings, then Storage and data, then Manage storage, where you can review and delete the largest files and the most space-hungry chats.

While you're there, look at the automatic download settings. Turning off automatic downloads for videos and documents, or limiting them to Wi-Fi, means files are only saved when you choose to open them. This stops the problem from coming straight back.

On an iPhone, the Messages app can also take a lot of space, especially with years of photos and videos in your conversations. In Settings, under Messages, the **Keep Messages** option lets you choose whether messages are kept forever, for a year or for 30 days. Changing it deletes older messages and their attachments permanently, so only use it if you're comfortable losing them, or once anything important has been saved elsewhere.

## Remove apps you don't use

Most of us have apps we installed once and never opened again. Each one takes space for the app itself, and many also store data and downloads alongside it.

Go through your app list and delete the ones you don't use. You can always reinstall them later from the app store, and many apps keep your data in an account, so signing back in restores it. If an app holds something you care about that isn't stored in an account, like offline notes or game progress, check for an export or backup option first.

Your phone may also be able to help:

- On iPhone, the iPhone Storage screen can recommend **Offload Unused Apps**. Offloading removes the app itself but keeps its documents and data, so if you reinstall it later you pick up where you left off.
- On Android, some recent versions and the Google Play Store can archive apps you rarely use in a similar way, removing most of the app while keeping your data. The option's name and availability vary by phone.

Games deserve a special look. They can easily be several gigabytes each, and a single game you've finished with can free more space than hundreds of photos.

## Clear downloads and old files

Files you've downloaded tend to be forgotten the moment you've used them: PDFs, tickets, attachments, installers, documents shared with you. They sit in the Downloads folder for years.

Open your file manager, go to Downloads, and sort by size or date. Delete anything you no longer need. On an iPhone, the Files app works the same way for files stored on the phone.

While you're in the file manager, look out for other large files you might have forgotten, such as screen recordings, old videos saved outside your gallery, and voice recordings. Many file managers have a view that lists the largest files on the phone, which makes these easy to find.

## Clear app caches

Apps store temporary files, called a cache, to load things faster. Browsers keep copies of web pages, social apps keep images, streaming apps keep bits of video. Over time, caches can grow to a surprising size.

On Android, you can clear an app's cache from Settings: open Apps, choose an app, tap Storage, and then Clear cache. This removes the temporary files without affecting your data. Be careful not to tap Clear storage or Clear data by mistake. That resets the app completely and can delete things saved inside it.

On an iPhone, there's no general button to clear a single app's cache. Some apps have their own option to clear cached data in their settings. Otherwise, offloading and reinstalling an app, or deleting and reinstalling one whose data lives in an account, has a similar effect.

Clearing caches is useful, but expect the space to come back gradually as you use the apps again. That's normal. Caches are part of how apps stay quick.

## Check offline downloads and media

Streaming apps make it easy to download things to watch or listen to offline, and just as easy to forget they're there. A few downloaded series, playlists or podcast episodes can take up many gigabytes.

Check the downloads section in each streaming, music, video and podcast app you use, and remove anything you've already watched or listened to. Many of these apps have an option to delete downloads automatically once you've finished them. It's worth turning on.

Don't forget offline maps and navigation apps, which can store large map areas for places you visited once, and reading apps, which may keep downloaded books and magazines.

## Know what not to delete

When you're in a hurry to free space, it's easy to delete things that look like clutter but aren't. A few kinds of file are worth leaving alone, or at least checking carefully first.

- Anything in a folder you don't recognise inside Android's system or app folders. Apps store their working files in their own folders, and deleting them by hand from a file manager can break the app or lose its data. Use the app's own settings, or the Clear cache option described above, instead.
- Photos and videos that aren't backed up yet. If your backup hasn't finished, wait. A full phone is an inconvenience; a lost photo can't be undone.
- Offline files you rely on when travelling, such as boarding passes, tickets and maps you'll need without a signal.
- Two-factor authentication apps and their data. Deleting an authenticator app, or clearing its storage, removes the codes you use to sign in to important accounts. Never remove one to save space.
- Voice notes and recordings that may be the only copy of something, like a message from a family member.

If you're unsure about a file, move it to a computer or cloud storage rather than deleting it outright. You can always delete it later once you know you don't need it.

## Restart and update your phone

Two simple things can free a little space and make your phone run better, and they only take a few minutes.

Restart the phone. Phones build up temporary files as they run, and some of these are only cleared on a restart. It won't free gigabytes, but it can help the storage figures update properly after a big clear-out, and it's a good habit anyway.

Install pending updates, then clear the leftovers. A downloaded but uninstalled update can sit on your phone taking up space. Installing it lets the phone tidy up the download afterwards. If an update won't install because there isn't enough room, clear a little space first using the steps above, then try again.

Once you've restarted, go back to the storage screen from the first step and compare. The comparison shows which steps made the biggest difference for your phone, so you know where to look next time.

## Move large files off the phone

If you've cleared everything you don't want and still need more room, the next step is to move things you do want to keep somewhere else.

Cloud storage is the most convenient option. Once your photos and videos are safely backed up, many photo services let you remove the full-size copies from your phone while keeping them available online. Check that you understand how your service works first, and remember that you'll need an internet connection to see those photos.

A computer is a good home for things you rarely need on the go, such as old videos, finished projects or large archives. Copy them across, check they open on the computer, and only then delete them from the phone.

A memory card is an option on some Android phones, though many newer phones no longer have a card slot. If yours does, you can move photos, videos and some files to it. Remember that a memory card is not a backup: it lives in the same phone and can be lost or damaged with it.

## Keep your phone from filling up again

Once you've cleared space, a few habits keep it that way without needing another big clear-out:

- Leave some breathing room. Try to keep around 10 to 15 percent of your storage free. Phones need spare space for updates, temporary files and new photos, and tend to run more smoothly with it.
- Limit automatic downloads in chat apps to the file types you want, or to Wi-Fi only.
- Turn on automatic removal of watched episodes and listened-to podcasts in streaming apps.
- Keep your backup running, so you can clear photos from the phone whenever you need to without worrying.
- Do a five-minute tidy once a month. Clear screenshots, empty the downloads folder and delete apps you haven't used.
