## Why documents end up on your phone

A few years ago, documents lived on computers. Now, for many people, the phone is where they arrive first. A contract comes by email. A school newsletter is shared in a group chat. A bank statement, a ticket, a timetable or an invoice is downloaded from a website. A colleague sends a spreadsheet or a set of slides and asks you to take a quick look.

The trouble is that these files come in different formats, made by different programs, and a phone doesn't always know what to do with each one. You tap an attachment and get a message saying there's no app to open it, or you're sent to download a different app for every type of file.

Document Reader: Read All PDF is built to solve exactly that: one app that opens PDF, Word, Excel and PowerPoint files on your Android phone, so you don't need a different app for each format. This guide explains those four formats, where documents are stored on your phone, and some simple habits for reading and keeping them.

## The four document types you'll meet most

Almost every document you receive will be one of four types. Knowing what each is for makes it easier to understand why they look and behave the way they do.

| Type | Common file endings | What it's usually used for |
|---|---|---|
| PDF | .pdf | Finished documents meant to look the same everywhere: forms, statements, tickets, manuals |
| Word | .doc, .docx | Letters, reports and other text documents that are still being written or edited |
| Excel | .xls, .xlsx | Spreadsheets: tables of numbers, lists, budgets and schedules |
| PowerPoint | .ppt, .pptx | Presentations made up of slides |

### PDF

PDF stands for Portable Document Format. It was created by Adobe in the early 1990s and later became an open international standard. Its big idea is that a PDF looks the same on every device: the same fonts, the same layout, the same page breaks. That's why official documents, forms, receipts and tickets are so often sent as PDFs. The person sending it knows exactly what you'll see.

The trade-off is that a PDF is laid out as fixed pages. On a small phone screen, a page designed for A4 paper can be hard to read without zooming in.

### Word documents

Word documents are text documents made in Microsoft Word or a compatible program. The older format ends in .doc; the newer one, used since Office 2007, ends in .docx. Unlike a PDF, a Word document is designed to be edited, so its layout can shift slightly depending on the device and the fonts available.

### Excel spreadsheets

Excel files hold spreadsheets: grids of cells arranged in rows and columns, often with several sheets inside one file. As with Word, older files end in .xls and newer ones in .xlsx. Spreadsheets are often much wider than a phone screen, which makes navigating them the main challenge on mobile.

### PowerPoint presentations

PowerPoint files are presentations made of slides, ending in .ppt for older files and .pptx for newer ones. Because each slide is designed to be shown full screen, presentations are often easiest to read on a phone turned sideways.

## Where documents are saved on your phone

One of the most common frustrations is downloading a document and then not being able to find it again. On Android, where a file ends up depends on how it arrived.

- **Downloaded from a website:** usually saved to the **Downloads** folder.
- **Attached to an email:** often opened straight from the email app, and saved to Downloads only if you choose to save it.
- **Sent in a chat app:** usually kept inside that app's own media folder, and sometimes also visible in your phone's file manager.
- **Stored in a cloud drive:** kept online, and only saved to your phone if you download it or make it available offline.

If you can't find a document, your phone's file manager app is the best place to start. Most file managers let you browse by folder, filter by file type, such as documents, and search by name.

## Opening documents from other apps

You don't always have to go looking for a document to open it. On Android, when you tap a file in an email, a chat or a file manager, your phone asks which app should open it, or uses the app you picked last time. You can also use the **Share** or **Open with** option in most apps to send a document to the app you want to read it in.

If a document keeps opening in the wrong app, you can usually change this in your phone's settings, under the apps section, where each app's default settings can be cleared. The next time you open that kind of file, your phone will ask again which app to use.

## Reading comfortably on a small screen

Phone screens are small, and most documents weren't designed for them. A few simple habits make a big difference.

- **Turn your phone sideways** for wide documents, spreadsheets and slides. Landscape mode gives each line more room.
- **Pinch to zoom** on a specific part of a page rather than trying to read the whole page at once.
- **Increase your screen brightness** or use a dark theme in low light, whichever is easier on your eyes.
- **Take breaks with long documents.** Reading dozens of pages on a phone is tiring. For long contracts or reports, a larger screen may be worth waiting for.

### Spreadsheets on a phone

Spreadsheets are the hardest type to read on a phone because they're usually wider than the screen. It helps to look first at the sheet names, which are often shown as tabs, so you know how the file is organised. Then move around in small steps rather than zooming right out, which makes numbers too small to read.

### Presentations on a phone

Read presentations one slide at a time, with the phone turned sideways. Some presentations also include speaker notes, which contain extra detail the presenter would have said out loud.

## Why a document might look different from what you expected

Sometimes a document opens but doesn't look quite right. Text is in a different font, a table has shifted, or a page break is in a strange place. This usually isn't a sign that anything is broken.

- **Missing fonts.** Word, Excel and PowerPoint files use the fonts installed on the computer they were made on. If your phone doesn't have the same font, a similar one is used instead, and line lengths can change slightly.
- **Older formats.** Very old .doc, .xls and .ppt files sometimes use features that newer software handles differently.
- **Complex layouts.** Documents with many columns, text boxes or embedded objects are harder to display exactly on a small screen.

If exact layout matters, for example with a form or a contract, ask the sender for a PDF. That's what the format is for.

## PDF or Word: which should you send?

If you need to send a document to someone else from your phone, it's worth thinking for a moment about which format to use, because the choice affects what the other person can do with it.

**Send a PDF when the document is finished.** Contracts, invoices, receipts, forms you've filled in, CVs and anything that needs to look exactly right should usually be sent as a PDF. The other person sees the same layout you do, whatever device they use, and it's harder to change by accident.

**Send a Word document when someone needs to edit it.** If you're asking a colleague to add comments, fill in a section or rewrite part of a draft, a Word document is easier for them to work with.

**Send a spreadsheet when the numbers need to be worked with.** A PDF of a spreadsheet is fine for reading a total, but if someone needs to sort, filter or update figures, send the original spreadsheet file.

When in doubt, ask the person what they need. Many people are happy to receive both: a PDF to read and the original file to edit.

## Common problems and simple fixes

Most problems with documents on a phone have simple causes. Here are the ones people run into most often.

- **"No app can open this file."** The file is in a format your phone doesn't recognise. Check the file ending. If it's a PDF, Word, Excel or PowerPoint file, a document reader app can open it.
- **The file won't download.** Check your internet connection and the space left on your phone. Large attachments may need Wi-Fi, and a full phone can't save new files.
- **The document is blank or shows strange characters.** The file may be damaged, or may not have finished downloading. Try downloading it again, or ask the sender to resend it.
- **The document asks for a password.** Some files are protected. The sender should give you the password separately; see the section on staying safe below.
- **You can't find a file you just downloaded.** Check the Downloads folder in your file manager, or sort files by date so the newest appear first.

## Reading documents offline

Phones aren't always connected. On a plane, on a train through a tunnel or abroad without mobile data, a document that only lives online can't be opened. If you'll need a document while travelling, save it to your phone before you set off.

Good candidates for saving ahead of time include boarding passes, booking confirmations, tickets, maps and itineraries, insurance details and any directions you'll need on arrival. Once a document is saved on the phone rather than just in an email or online drive, it can be opened whether or not you have a signal.

It's also worth keeping a second copy of travel documents somewhere you can reach from another device, such as a cloud drive or an email to yourself, in case the phone is lost or its battery runs flat at the wrong moment.

## Keeping your documents organised

A phone that collects documents for months quickly becomes hard to search. A little organisation keeps important files easy to find.

- **Rename files you'll need again.** "Car insurance 2026.pdf" is far easier to find later than "doc_83421.pdf".
- **Use a few simple folders**, such as Home, Work, Travel and Receipts, rather than leaving everything in Downloads.
- **Delete what you've finished with.** Tickets for past events and attachments you've already read just take up space.
- **Keep a copy of important documents off the phone**, in a cloud drive or on a computer, so they're safe if the phone is lost.

## Documents worth keeping a copy of

Some documents are easy to find again if you lose them. Others are a real nuisance to replace. It's worth keeping copies of the important ones somewhere safe, away from your phone.

Documents people most often wish they'd kept include:

- **Proof of identity and travel documents**, such as scans of a passport, a driver's licence or visas, stored securely.
- **Insurance policies and warranty documents**, which you'll need when something goes wrong, often when you're least prepared.
- **Receipts for expensive purchases**, for returns, warranty claims and insurance.
- **Tenancy agreements, contracts and official letters**, which can matter years later.
- **Medical letters and prescriptions**, especially when travelling.

For documents like these, keep at least one copy in secure cloud storage or on a computer, and think about who else in your household might need to find them in an emergency. Treat scans of identity documents with care: store them somewhere protected by a strong password, and delete copies you no longer need.

## Staying safe with documents

Documents are one of the ways scammers try to reach people, so it's worth a moment's caution before opening a file.

- **Be careful with attachments you weren't expecting**, especially from people or organisations you don't recognise.
- **Check the sender's address**, not just their display name. Scam emails often use names that look official.
- **Don't enter passwords or bank details** into a document or a link inside one because it asks you to.
- **Keep your phone's software up to date**, since updates often fix security problems.

Some documents, especially statements and official letters, are protected with a password. The sender will normally tell you the password separately, often something like your date of birth or part of an account number. If you don't know it, contact the sender directly rather than replying to the message the document came in.
