## Why phone backups matter

Your phone probably holds more of your life than any other thing you own. Years of photos and videos, the contact details of everyone you know, and data inside the apps you use every day, all stored on a device you carry everywhere, drop occasionally and replace every few years.

Phones stop working for all sorts of ordinary reasons. They get lost or stolen. They fall into water, or onto concrete. Batteries swell, screens die, and storage chips occasionally fail without warning. Software updates can go wrong, and a phone that won't start may need a factory reset to recover, which erases everything on it. And sometimes the problem isn't the phone at all: it's a photo deleted by mistake, or an album cleared to make space.

In every one of those situations, the question is the same: is there a copy of what was on the phone somewhere else? If there is, losing the phone is an inconvenience. If there isn't, it can mean losing things that can't be replaced, like photos of people and places that only exist on that one device.

A backup is simply that copy. It doesn't prevent anything from going wrong with your phone, but it changes what happens next. Instead of trying to recover data from a broken or missing device, you restore it from your backup onto a phone that works.

## What's worth backing up

Not everything on your phone needs the same level of care. Apps can be downloaded again, settings can be set up again, and music and films from streaming services live on those services, not on your phone. What matters most is the information that only exists because you created it or saved it.

### Photos and videos

For most people, the camera roll is the most valuable and least replaceable thing on the phone. It's also usually the largest. Photos and videos are the reason most people start backing up in the first place, and the thing they most regret losing when they didn't.

### Contacts

Contacts are easy to take for granted until they're gone. Some are stored in an online account and some only on the phone itself, and many people don't know which is which until they switch phones and find names missing. A backup of your contacts means a new phone doesn't mean rebuilding your address book from memory.

### App data

App data is the information stored inside the apps you use: the content, settings and progress that make an app yours. Some apps keep this data online in an account, but many keep at least some of it only on the phone. It's the category people most often forget, and the one that's hardest to rebuild by hand.

Cloud Storage Backup & Drive is built around exactly these four things: your photos, videos, contacts and app data. You choose what to back up, send it to the cloud, and restore it when you need it.

## Backup, sync and storage: what's the difference?

These three words get used almost interchangeably, but they mean different things, and the difference matters when something goes wrong.

| | What it does | What happens if you delete something |
|---|---|---|
| **Backup** | Keeps a separate copy of your data | The backup copy is still there to restore |
| **Sync** | Keeps the same data identical across devices | The deletion is usually copied everywhere |
| **Storage** | Gives you space to keep files | Depends on how you use it |
{: .guide-table}

**Sync** is designed for convenience. When you add a contact on your phone, sync makes it appear on your tablet and computer too. But the same logic applies in reverse: delete a contact on one device, and sync removes it from the others. Sync on its own protects you from losing a device, but not always from losing data.

A **backup** is designed for recovery. It keeps a copy of your data separately, so that if something is lost, deleted or damaged on the phone, you can bring it back.

**Cloud storage** is simply space online where files can live. It's the place a cloud backup is kept, and it can also be somewhere you choose to keep files so they don't have to take up room on the phone.

## Where your backup should live

The whole point of a backup is that it survives when the phone doesn't. That means it needs to be somewhere other than the phone itself.

A memory card inside the phone isn't a real backup in that sense. If the phone is lost, stolen or damaged, the card usually goes with it, and memory cards can fail too. A copy on a computer is better, but only if you remember to update it, and a computer can fail or be lost as well.

Cloud backup solves both problems. Your backup is stored online, away from the phone, so it isn't affected by anything that happens to the device. And because it works over the internet, you don't need to plug anything in or remember to carry a cable.

If you want to be extra careful, a good rule of thumb is to keep at least two copies of anything irreplaceable, in two different places. A cloud backup plus a copy on a computer at home covers almost every situation that could realistically go wrong.

## Making your first backup

The first backup always takes the longest, because everything on the phone has to be copied for the first time. Later backups are usually quicker, because far less changes between one backup and the next.

A few simple habits make the first backup go smoothly:

- **Use Wi-Fi.** Photo and video libraries can be many gigabytes. Backing up over mobile data can use up a data allowance quickly.
- **Keep the phone charged.** A long backup on a low battery may pause or stop. Plugging the phone in while it backs up avoids that.
- **Tidy up first.** Deleting duplicate photos, blurry shots and videos you don't want makes the backup smaller and faster.
- **Give it time.** If you have years of photos, let the backup run, and check back later rather than assuming it finished straight away.

Once the first backup is done, keep your backup up to date. A backup is only as useful as its most recent copy: photos taken after your last backup aren't in it.

## How to check your backup actually works

It's surprisingly common for people to believe their phone is backed up, only to discover when they need it that the backup stopped months ago, or never included the things that mattered most. A few minutes of checking avoids that.

- **Look at what's included.** Confirm the backup covers the photos, videos, contacts and app data you care about, not just some of them.
- **Look at the date.** Check when your backup last completed. If it's weeks or months ago, find out why.
- **Spot-check a few items.** Pick some photos from different years, and a handful of contacts, and confirm they're in your backup.
- **Try restoring something small.** The best proof that a backup works is restoring from it. Try restoring a single item you don't mind about, so you know how it works before you need it for real.

Checking your backup every month or two, and always before any big change like a software update or a new phone, takes very little time and gives you real confidence that your data is safe.

## How often should you back up?

The honest answer is: often enough that losing everything since your last backup wouldn't upset you. For most people, that means keeping backups going on a regular basis rather than doing one big backup a year.

Think about how quickly new things arrive on your phone. If you take photos most days, a month without a backup could mean hundreds of pictures that exist only on the phone. If you rarely take photos but add contacts and use apps that store data on the device, your backup still falls behind over time, just more slowly.

A few moments are especially worth backing up before, whatever your usual routine:

- **Before a major software update.** Updates rarely go wrong, but when they do, a recent backup turns a disaster into an inconvenience.
- **Before a repair.** Phones sent away for repair are sometimes reset or replaced, and repair shops generally won't guarantee your data.
- **Before a holiday or big event**, when you'll take lots of photos and the phone is more likely to be lost or damaged.
- **Before switching phones**, as covered below.

## Common backup mistakes

Most lost photos aren't lost because people never thought about backing up. They're lost because of a small mistake that went unnoticed until it was too late. These are the most common ones.

- **Assuming it's working.** A backup that was set up once and never checked may have stopped months ago, after a change of password or a full cloud account.
- **Backing up only part of the phone.** Many people back up their camera folder but not screenshots, downloaded pictures or the photos saved by chat apps.
- **Keeping the only copy on the phone.** A second folder or a memory card inside the same phone is lost along with it.
- **Deleting before checking.** Clearing photos from the phone to make space, and only later discovering they never finished uploading.
- **Forgetting the account.** A backup is only useful if you can sign in to it. Make sure you know the password for the account your backup is stored in, and that you can still receive its verification codes.

Each of these takes only a moment to avoid, and the checks in the section above catch almost all of them.

## Restoring onto a new phone

Restoring is the other half of backing up, and it's where a backup proves its worth. With Cloud Storage Backup & Drive, you can bring what you've backed up back onto your phone whenever you need it, including after you switch to a new one.

When you're moving to a new phone, the order you do things in matters. Back up the old phone first and confirm the backup is complete. Then set up the new phone, install the app, and restore your photos, videos, contacts and app data. Only once you've checked that everything you need is on the new phone should you reset the old one.

## Keeping your phone's storage under control

A full phone is slow, can't take new photos and may refuse to install updates. Once your photos and videos are safely backed up, you have more freedom in how you manage that space.

Before you delete anything from your phone to make room, make sure it really is in your backup: check the backup has completed, and open a few of the items you're about to remove. Deleting from the phone first and checking afterwards is how photos get lost.

Other good ways to free space without losing anything:

- Delete apps you no longer use. Most can be reinstalled later if you change your mind.
- Clear old downloads, such as documents and attachments you've already read.
- Remove duplicate and near-identical photos, like bursts of the same shot.
- Check chat apps, which often save every photo and video you're sent, even ones you've looked at once.

## Keeping your backup private

A backup is a copy of some of your most personal information, so it's worth protecting the account it lives in as carefully as the phone itself.

- **Use a strong, unique password** for your account, one you don't use anywhere else.
- **Turn on two-factor authentication** wherever an account offers it, so a stolen password alone isn't enough to get in.
- **Lock your phone** with a PIN, pattern, fingerprint or face unlock, so someone who picks it up can't open your apps.
- **Be wary of messages asking for your login details.** Genuine services don't ask for your password by email or text.
